Without these the record belongs to the model. Add a plate or VIN and it belongs to that one car — which is what makes handing it on mean anything. Filling the rest in automatically from the plate is a server job, not something this page can do — see Method.

On proving any of this. Everything above is entered by whoever is holding the phone, so it is a claim, not a fact — which is why every stamp shows as unverified and nothing here pretends otherwise. Photos stay in this browser and are never uploaded anywhere.

On average repair costs. The averages above are yours alone — your receipts, divided your way. A trustworthy benchmark for what a brake job on an Ioniq 5 should cost cannot be conjured from one owner's history; it needs a pool of them, which is the real asset a service like this would accumulate. Every receipt logged makes the next owner's comparison better, and that is worth being straight with people about rather than showing an invented national average.

Real verification does not come from a photo. It comes from the workshop that did the work: a franchised dealer network will confirm a car's digital service record against its VIN, and in Denmark the periodic inspection history is already public through Synsbasen. The credible product is to check the claim against those two sources and show a green stamp only when they agree — with an odometer that never runs backwards being the single strongest signal that a history is honest.

Method

Where every number on the other tabs comes from, and what it is worth.

  • Factory settings are derived, not typed in. Each car's baseline consumption comes from its own published battery and WLTP range — battery ÷ range. The physics model is then calibrated so it reproduces exactly that figure at a steady 80 km/h and 20 °C, which is roughly what the WLTP cycle corresponds to. That is why every car's paper range is reproduced exactly at the reference condition, and diverges honestly away from it.
  • The speed curve is physics. Rolling resistance scales with weight and stays flat with speed; aerodynamic drag scales with frontal area and the square of speed, so the power it demands scales with the cube. Cabin load is a fixed wattage spread over fewer kilometres the faster you go.
  • Cold is charged twice. Below 15 °C the model adds cabin heating, stiffens the tyres slightly, and shaves usable battery capacity — up to 14% at the bottom of the scale.
  • Charging speed is capped by the car. Each car carries its own peak DC rate, so a 350 kW charger will not make a 100 kW car charge faster. It assumes 90% efficiency and a constant rate; real DC charging tapers hard above roughly 60% state of charge, so read it as a best case for the bottom half of the battery.
  • A plate could fill this in for you — but not from here. In Denmark a registration number resolves to the exact variant, first registration date and full inspection history through the Motorregister and Synsbasen, and several commercial APIs wrap them. None of it can happen in this page: a browser cannot call those services directly (they block cross-origin requests), and an API key put into a public page is a key you have given away. It needs a small server of your own that holds the key and answers plate lookups — a day's work, and the point at which this stops being a static file. Until then the plate is an identifier you type, and everything else is entered by hand.
  • The service record lives in the browser that typed it. Stamps are saved against the plate or VIN, so they survive a refresh and each car keeps its own history. They are not on a server, which means they do not follow you to another phone — use Hand over to export the record and Import to load it somewhere else. Photos are never included; they stay on the device that added them. Real accounts that carry a car's history between owners need that same server.
  • Prices are Danish list prices, and only some are sourced. Sixteen of them were corrected against published 2025 figures — among others the Citroën ë-C3 at 139.990, Cupra Born at 249.990, Volvo EX30 at 244.990, Škoda Enyaq and VW ID.4 at 304.995, BMW i5 at 564.900 and Audi Q6 e-tron at 685.390. The rest are estimates, and Danish EV prices have fallen roughly 10–15% since 2023, so treat any unsourced figure as high rather than low. Because no hand-written table survives contact with a real market, price is editable: open Adjust spec and paste the actual asking price from Bilbasen or a dealer, and every cost on the Costs tab follows it.
  • The catalogue is 136 models across 40 brands, and most of it is estimated. Battery and WLTP range are the two figures that matter most, because every consumption number is derived from them, and those are close to the published specs. Weights, drag areas and charging rates are reasoned estimates; they shape the speed curve rather than the headline. Only sixteen prices are sourced. All of it is good enough to rank cars honestly against each other and not good enough to sign a contract on — which is why every one of these fields is editable under Adjust spec. A production version would pull the lot from a maintained feed.
